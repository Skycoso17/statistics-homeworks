Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

Why a simple bell curve leads to a non-elementary integral, and how numerical methods turn that integral into useful probabilities.

01

Starting from the meaning, not from the formula

The goal of this homework is to compute the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

Before thinking about algorithms, we need to understand what the CDF means. The normal density is the familiar bell-shaped curve. The total area under that curve is 1, because the whole curve represents all possible probability.

Density φ(x)

“How concentrated is probability around x?”

The density describes the shape of the bell curve. A higher curve means that values around that point are more concentrated.

CDF Φ(z)

“How much probability has accumulated before z?”

The CDF is the entire area under the curve to the left of z.

φ(x) = (1 / √(2π)) e−x²/2

Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z) = ∫−∞z φ(x) dx

The main idea: computing Φ(z) is an area problem. The difficulty is that this particular area cannot be obtained from an ordinary elementary antiderivative.

Why is the CDF an area and not simply the height of the curve?

For a continuous random variable, the probability of obtaining one exact value is zero. The height of the density at a point therefore does not represent the probability of that exact point. Probability comes from an interval, and intervals correspond to areas under the density curve.

This is why the CDF accumulates area from the far left up to z: it converts the shape of the density into an actual probability.

02

Why is the normal density a probability distribution?

A probability density must be non-negative and its total area must be 1. The Gaussian function is always positive, and the normalization constant 1/√(2π) is chosen precisely so that the full area becomes 1.

∫−∞∞ (1/√(2π))e−x²/2 dx = 1

The notes supplied with the assignment derive this Gaussian integral using a two-dimensional integral and polar coordinates. The important conceptual point for this homework is simpler: the bell curve has been scaled so that its complete area is exactly one probability unit.

03

What changes when we move z?

Imagine moving a vertical line from the far left of the bell curve to the far right. The area accumulated on the left can only increase.

z very negative→ almost no area collected→ z = 0 gives half the curve→ z very positive gives almost all the area

Because the standard normal curve is symmetric around zero:

Φ(0)=0.5     and     Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z)

Why symmetry matters numerically: we do not need to integrate from negative infinity every time. We can compute the area between 0 and |z| and then add or subtract it from 0.5.

What information does Φ(z) give us?

A single CDF value summarizes everything to the left of z. For example, if Φ(1)=0.8413, this means that about 84.13% of the standard normal distribution lies below z=1.

So the CDF is not merely another curve: it is a direct probability calculator. This is why it is used for percentiles, hypothesis tests, confidence intervals and p-values.

04

The real mathematical obstacle

At first sight, the problem seems like an ordinary calculus exercise: integrate e−x²/2. But no substitution, integration by parts, logarithm or trigonometric identity produces an elementary antiderivative.

This is not just a historical failure to find the right trick. A classical result associated with Liouville's theorem on elementary antiderivatives tells us that the antiderivative of the Gaussian cannot be expressed as a finite combination of elementary functions.

What does “not elementary” mean?

It means we cannot write the answer using only a finite combination of familiar objects such as polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions, inverse trigonometric functions and algebraic operations.

The integral still exists. We simply need a larger mathematical vocabulary.

A little more mathematics: what is Liouville telling us?

Liouville's theory gives a structural test for when an elementary function can have an elementary antiderivative. The Gaussian exponential fails that test. Therefore an expression built only from ordinary elementary functions cannot represent its indefinite integral.

For this homework, the important conclusion is not the technical proof: it is that numerical computation is necessary for a structural mathematical reason, not because the Gaussian is poorly understood.

Logic: the integral exists → no elementary antiderivative exists → we introduce a special function and numerical algorithms.

Why is this important for numerical computation?

If an elementary antiderivative existed, we could evaluate Φ(z) simply by substituting the endpoints into a closed formula. Because it does not exist, every practical computation must somehow approximate the required area or approximate a special function that represents it.

In other words, numerical computation is not an optional shortcut here: it is part of the normal workflow for evaluating the CDF.

05

Why introduce the error function?

If an integral appears constantly in mathematics, science and statistics, it is useful to give that integral its own name. That is exactly what happens with the error function.

erf(x) = (2/√π) ∫0x e−t² dt

After a simple change of variables, the normal CDF can be written exactly as:

Φ(z) = ½ [1 + erf(z/√2)]

This is an exact analytical representation. But erf is itself a special function defined from the same kind of non-elementary integral.

Special functions solve the representation problem.
Numerical methods solve the evaluation problem.

Why introduce a new function instead of just keeping the integral?

Naming the recurring integral gives us a reusable mathematical object. Once erf is defined, many formulas in probability, diffusion, heat flow and statistics become shorter and easier to manipulate.

The important point is that “exact analytical representation” and “easy numerical evaluation” are different goals. erf solves the first one; algorithms solve the second.

06

Where do the benchmark numbers come from?

This homework does not use a randomly generated dataset. We select several meaningful z-values across the center and tails of the normal distribution and attach a benchmark CDF value to each one. These benchmark values come from a standard normal table and are stored locally in reference-data.csv.

The NIST table reports the area between 0 and |z|, not Φ(z) directly. Therefore we reconstruct the CDF using symmetry:

z ≥ 0: Φ(z)=0.5+A(z)      z < 0: Φ(z)=0.5−A(|z|)

Example: z = 1.53

NIST reports the area from 0 to 1.53 as 0.43699.

Φ(1.53)=0.5+0.43699=0.93699

The file reference-data.csv contains the benchmark values used by the experiment. Keeping them in a separate file makes the comparison reproducible and makes it clear that the algorithms are being tested against fixed external target values rather than against each other.

Why do we need benchmark values at all?

If three algorithms produce similar numbers, that does not automatically prove that they are correct. They could all share the same mistake.

A trusted benchmark gives us an independent target. Accuracy is then measured by the distance between our computed value and that target.

07

Three ways to compute the same CDF

We now deliberately solve the same problem in three different ways. The goal is not only to obtain the right number, but to understand the trade-off between mathematical simplicity, numerical accuracy and computational cost.

1 · Simpson's rule

Compute the probability area directly. We divide the interval between 0 and |z| into small pieces and approximate the curve locally.

2 · Power series

Replace erf by an infinite series and keep adding terms until the new contribution is tiny.

3 · erf approximation

Use a compact approximation designed specifically for erf (Abramowitz–Stegun 7.1.26).

Why compare different methods if they all approximate the same value?

Numerical methods are judged by more than correctness. We also care about how quickly the error decreases, how much computation is required, and whether the method behaves well in different regions of the input.

That is why this homework compares accuracy, convergence and efficiency separately.

08

Method 1 · Simpson's rule

Numerical integration is the most direct translation of the definition of Φ. We approximate the area instead of searching for an antiderivative.

Using symmetry, we only need:

A(z)=∫0|z| φ(x) dx

Simpson's rule approximates two adjacent pieces of the curve using a parabola. Because the normal density is very smooth, this works extremely well.

∫ f(x)dx ≈ (h/3)[f(x₀)+4f(x₁)+2f(x₂)+⋯+4f(xₙ₋₁)+f(xₙ)]

Concept: we do not need an antiderivative if we can approximate the area accurately enough.

What controls the accuracy?

The number of subintervals N controls how finely we follow the bell curve. A larger N means smaller pieces and usually a smaller integration error, but also more evaluations of the density.

09

Method 2 · Power series

Instead of approximating the area geometrically, we can approximate the special function algebraically.

erf(x) = (2/√π) Σ [ (−1)nx2n+1 / (n!(2n+1)) ]

This is an infinite series. A computer evaluates only finitely many terms and stops when the next term is smaller than a chosen tolerance.

Near zero

Terms become small quickly. The method is efficient.

For larger |z|

More terms are required before the additions become negligible.

Numerical lesson

A mathematically convergent series is not equally efficient at every input value.

What does “convergent series” mean in practice?

It means that if we keep adding more terms, the partial sum approaches the exact value. But the rate of approach matters computationally: a slowly converging series may be mathematically correct and still be a poor algorithm.

10

Method 3 · A practical erf approximation

If we need Φ millions of times, repeatedly integrating a curve is wasteful. A more practical approach is to approximate erf directly.

This prototype uses the classic Abramowitz–Stegun formula 7.1.26, a short expression involving an exponential and a fifth-degree polynomial-like term. Its published maximum absolute error for erf is about 1.5 × 10−7.

Concept: specialized approximations use knowledge about the function's shape to get a good answer with a fixed, small number of operations.

Why can this be faster than direct integration?

Simpson's rule evaluates the normal density many times for each new z. A compact erf approximation instead performs a short, fixed sequence of arithmetic operations. That fixed cost becomes very important when the CDF is evaluated repeatedly.

Why do we care about convergence and not only one good result?

18

Final conclusion

The normal CDF looks like a simple area under a familiar curve, but it reveals an important distinction between analytical mathematics and numerical computation.

Liouville's result explains why we cannot simplify the Gaussian integral into elementary functions. The error function gives us an exact special-function representation. Numerical methods then give us practical values.

The final lesson:
we know exactly what Φ means mathematically;
we know why elementary calculus cannot express it;
and we can still compute it to very high accuracy.
