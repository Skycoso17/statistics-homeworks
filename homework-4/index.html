Stochastic Penetration Process

From random penetration attempts to predictable large-scale behavior: Bernoulli and Rademacher models, Law of Large Numbers, variance scaling and Central Limit Theorem.

01

Starting point and purpose of the project

Suppose we want to describe the activity of several attackers against a collection of systems. The result of a single penetration step is uncertain, so it is natural to represent it with a random variable Xi.

Instead of trying to predict every single attack, the goal of this homework is to understand what happens when the number of systems becomes large. We want to see whether random behavior remains completely unpredictable or whether regular statistical patterns emerge.

For every attacker we observe a sequence X1, X2, ..., Xn of independent and identically distributed increments. We then study the accumulated score, its average and a square-root normalization.

SnTotal score
Sn/nAverage score
Sn/√nNormalized total score

Bernoulli model

Xi ∈ {0,1}

1 means successful penetration.
0 means unsuccessful penetration.

The parameter p is the probability of success at each step.

Rademacher model

Xi ∈ {-1,+1}

+1 means progress for the attacker.
-1 means an equally strong negative step.

This model is symmetric, so its expected increment is zero.

02

How to read n and m

n = systems / penetration steps

n controls the length of one attack process. Increasing n lets us study long-run behavior and is what makes the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem visible.

m = attackers / independent repetitions

m is the number of independent trajectories that we simulate. Increasing m does not change the theoretical distribution of one attacker; it gives us a more accurate empirical picture of that distribution.

What we want to compare

For increasing n we compare the behavior of Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n, paying special attention to their means, variances and shapes.

Main question. Can a process made of unpredictable individual steps become predictable when viewed on the right scale? The following experiments answer this question progressively.

06

Before going further — What is variance?

The variance tells us how spread out the possible results are around their mean. It is a measure of uncertainty or variability.

Two random processes can have the same average but behave very differently. One may stay close to its mean most of the time, while the other may produce values that are often far away. The variance is what allows us to distinguish these two situations.

Mean

The mean tells us the center of the distribution: the value around which the results are balanced on average.

Variance

The variance tells us how far the observations typically spread around that center. A small variance means that the results are concentrated; a large variance means that they are more dispersed.

Why square the deviations?

Positive and negative deviations would otherwise cancel each other. Squaring them makes every deviation positive and gives more weight to large deviations.

Var(X) = E[(X - μ)²]

In words: we measure the distance between each possible value and the mean, square that distance, and then average those squared distances.

Why do we need variance in this homework? Because the three quantities Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n have very different variances. Studying those variances tells us whether randomness is growing, disappearing or remaining visible when n increases.

Simple example. In the Rademacher model the mean is 0, but the total score does not stay fixed at 0. Its variance is n, so the possible totals spread farther from zero as the experiment becomes longer. After dividing by n, instead, the variance becomes 1/n, so the average becomes more and more concentrated around 0.

07

Before the experiment — What is the Law of Large Numbers?

The Law of Large Numbers (LLN) explains why averages become stable when we repeat the same random experiment many times.

Suppose that X1, X2, ..., Xn are independent and identically distributed random variables with expected value E[X]=μ. Their sample average is

Sn/n = (X1 + X2 + ... + Xn)/n.

The Law of Large Numbers states that, as n becomes large, this empirical average becomes increasingly close to the theoretical mean:

Sn/n → μ   in probability.

Intuitive meaning

With only a few observations, the average can move a lot because every new result has a strong influence. With hundreds or thousands of observations, one additional result has a much smaller effect, so the average becomes more stable.

Bernoulli case

Here E[X]=p. Therefore the fraction of successful penetrations Sn/n should approach p. If p=0.30, a long experiment should show about 30% successes.

Rademacher case

Here E[X]=0. Therefore the average score should approach 0. This does not mean that the total score itself becomes zero; it means that the total becomes small compared with the number of steps.

Key idea. The LLN answers the question: where does the average go? It tells us about long-run stability, not about the detailed shape of the remaining random fluctuations.
08

Step 3 — Observe the Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers concerns the average Sn/n. We therefore follow the running average Sk/k along several trajectories and ask whether it stabilizes.

Theoretical expectation. For Bernoulli increments, E[X]=p, so the average should approach p. For Rademacher increments, E[X]=0, so the average should approach 0.

The important point is that convergence of the average does not mean that randomness disappears from the total score. It means that the total score becomes predictable relative to the number of steps.

09

Step 4 — Why the normalization changes the variance

If the increments have variance σ², independence gives Var(Sn) = nσ². From this one formula we can understand all three scales.

TotalVar(Sn) = nσ²grows linearly with n
AverageVar(Sn/n) = σ²/nshrinks to 0
NormalizedVar(Sn/√n) = σ²remains constant

This explains why the three graphs behave so differently. Dividing by n is strong enough to suppress fluctuations; dividing by √n exactly compensates for their natural growth.

10

Before the experiment — What is the Central Limit Theorem?

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) explains what the distribution of many accumulated random effects looks like when the number of independent steps becomes large.

The simplest idea first. Imagine repeating a random step many times and adding all the results. The exact total is still random, but after the right rescaling the collection of many possible totals begins to form the familiar bell shape of a normal distribution.

This is different from the Law of Large Numbers. The LLN tells us that the average becomes stable. The CLT tells us what happens to the remaining random fluctuations around that average.

1. Start from the total

We add the increments and obtain Sn. If n grows, this total can spread more and more because more random contributions are being accumulated.

2. Remove the expected part

The expected total is nμ. Subtracting it leaves only the random deviation from what we expected to happen.

3. Rescale the fluctuations

The natural size of those fluctuations is σ√n. Dividing by this quantity puts experiments with different n on the same scale.

(Sn − nμ) / (σ√n)   →   N(0,1)

What does each symbol mean? μ is the mean of one increment, σ is its standard deviation, nμ is the expected total after n steps, and σ√n is the natural scale of the random fluctuations.

What is N(0,1)? It is the standard normal distribution: a symmetric bell-shaped distribution with mean 0 and variance 1. Values near 0 are the most common, while very large positive or negative deviations are increasingly rare.

Why is this useful? It allows us to approximate a complicated distribution of accumulated random effects with a simple and well-known normal distribution. Once we have that approximation, probabilities, confidence ranges and unusual deviations become much easier to study.

Why is the Rademacher model especially convenient here? Because for one Rademacher increment we already have μ=0 and σ=1. Therefore the general formula simplifies to

Sn/√n   →   N(0,1).

What we will look for in the graph. For small n, the normalized score still has a visibly discrete distribution because each increment is only -1 or +1. As n grows, the histogram should become smoother and increasingly similar to the normal bell curve. The individual data do not become normal; it is the distribution of the normalized sum that becomes approximately normal.
11

Step 5 — Observe the Central Limit Theorem in the Rademacher case

The Rademacher model has mean 0 and variance 1. Therefore no centering or additional variance correction is needed: the Central Limit Theorem predicts that Sn/√n converges in distribution to N(0,1).

What does “convergence in distribution” mean here? It does not mean that every simulated value becomes 0. It means that the shape of the distribution of the normalized scores becomes increasingly close to the standard bell curve.
The bars are simulated Rademacher data; the line is the theoretical density of N(0,1). Try changing n from 10 to 1000: the discrete distribution progressively takes the shape of the normal curve.

Why is the Rademacher case special in this homework? Because E[X]=0 and Var(X)=1. For Bernoulli variables the corresponding CLT quantity would instead be (Sn-np)/√(np(1-p)).

12

Step 6 — What changes when we increase the number of attackers m?

The number of attackers m controls how many independent realizations we observe. A small m can produce noisy empirical statistics simply because the sample is small. A large m gives estimates that are usually much closer to the theoretical values.

This distinction is fundamental: increasing n changes the asymptotic regime of each stochastic process, while increasing m mainly improves how accurately we can observe that regime in a simulation.

What do we observe in the experiment as a whole, and why is it useful?

The purpose of the homework is not simply to generate random trajectories. It is to show that the same underlying random process looks very different depending on the scale at which we observe it.

Microscopic view

At the level of one step, the outcome is unpredictable. A Bernoulli attempt may succeed or fail; a Rademacher step may be +1 or -1. Statistics does not remove this uncertainty.

Macroscopic view

After many independent steps, regular patterns emerge. The average becomes predictable, the variance follows precise scaling laws, and the normalized fluctuations acquire a stable shape.

Why this matters

In real systems we often cannot predict a single event, but we still need to estimate aggregate risk. These results let us reason about expected penetration rates, uncertainty around those rates and the probability of unusually large deviations.

Role of n. Increasing n means observing a longer process. This is what reveals the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem: the average stabilizes and the normalized fluctuations approach a normal law.

Role of m. Increasing m means observing more independent attackers. It does not change the law of one attacker, but it gives us a cleaner histogram and more accurate empirical estimates of mean and variance.

Bernoulli interpretation. The long-run fraction of successful penetrations approaches p. Therefore, although we cannot know which specific systems will be penetrated, we can estimate the overall success rate when the number of systems is large.

Rademacher interpretation. There is no systematic drift because positive and negative steps are equally likely. Nevertheless, the accumulated score can wander far from zero. The typical size of this wandering is of order √n, which is exactly why the normalization by √n is the correct one for the CLT.

Central lesson. The experiment shows three complementary ideas: the mean describes the expected direction, the variance describes how uncertain the result is around that direction, and the CLT describes the shape of those fluctuations when many independent contributions are aggregated.
15

Conclusions

1. Individual steps remain random. Nothing in the simulation allows us to predict the next increment with certainty. The regularity appears only when many independent steps are aggregated.

2. The Law of Large Numbers controls the average. The quantity Sn/n converges in probability to the expected increment: p for Bernoulli and 0 for Rademacher.

3. Different normalizations reveal different phenomena. The variance of the total score grows like n, the variance of the average decays like 1/n, and the square-root normalization preserves a constant amount of variability.

4. The Central Limit Theorem describes the remaining fluctuations. In the Rademacher case, after dividing by √n, the distribution tends to the standard normal law even though each original increment takes only the values -1 and +1.

5. Cybersecurity interpretation. With more systems, the absolute aggregate score can become larger and more variable, but its average behavior becomes more predictable. With more attackers, the underlying probabilistic model is unchanged; we simply obtain a clearer estimate of the distribution of possible outcomes.

Final message. Randomness at the microscopic level does not prevent statistical regularity at the macroscopic level. This is exactly what the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem formalize.
