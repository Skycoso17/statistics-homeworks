Sampling Mean and Variance

From individual penetration scores to statistical inference: each hacker observes a random sample, Welford summarizes it online, and repeated hackers reveal the sampling behavior of mean and variance.

From Homework 4 to this experiment

In Homework 4 we studied a stochastic penetration process. We imagined many hackers attacking many systems and represented each hacker by a random trajectory. The important idea was that every hacker experiences a different random path, even though all hackers follow the same probabilistic model.

In that homework, the main object of interest was the evolution of the penetration process itself. Here we keep exactly the same structure — m hackers, each performing n penetration attempts — but we ask a new statistical question.

03

Where do 0.6, μ = 6 and σ² = 2.4 come from?

The exercise allows us to choose any fixed probability distribution on the interval 0–10. So before running the simulation we must define a precise probabilistic model.

We choose a Binomial distribution with 10 trials and success probability p = 0.6:

X ~ Binomial(10, 0.6)

This choice is not produced by the data and it is not estimated by the hackers. It is a modeling assumption that we decide in advance for the experiment. We could have chosen another value of p or even another distribution, but once the model is fixed, its theoretical mean and variance are fixed too.

How should we interpret p = 0.6?

Imagine that one penetration attempt is evaluated through 10 small independent criteria. Each criterion contributes one point when it is satisfied. For each criterion, we assume a probability of success equal to 0.6.

Therefore the final score is simply the number of successful criteria out of 10: it can be 0, 1, 2, ..., 10.

The number 0.6 is therefore the success probability of each elementary sub-test. Since there are 10 such sub-tests, the expected score is:

μ = E[X] = np = 10 · 0.6 = 6

This explains where the value 6 comes from. It is not an arbitrary target inserted at the end of the simulation. It is the theoretical population mean implied by the probability model.

In intuitive terms, if we repeated an enormous number of penetration attempts, the average score would approach 6. Individual scores may be smaller or larger, but 6 is the long-run center of the distribution.

For a Binomial random variable, the theoretical variance is:

σ² = Var(X) = np(1 − p)

Substituting our parameters:

σ² = 10 · 0.6 · 0.4 = 2.4

The factor 0.4 appears because 1-p = 1-0.6 = 0.4 is the probability that one elementary criterion fails.

Model parameter p = 0.6 Chosen in advance as the success probability of one elementary criterion.
Theoretical mean μ = 6 Expected score implied by the model.
Theoretical variance σ² = 2.4 True population dispersion implied by the same model.
Why are μ = 6 and σ² = 2.4 so important later? Because in a simulation we know the population model that generated the data. That gives us a rare advantage: we know the true answers in advance. We can therefore check whether the statistics computed from finite samples actually behave as good estimators should.
In a real inferential-statistics problem, μ and σ² are usually unknown. Here we know them only because we designed the population ourselves. The experiment is therefore a controlled way to test how sample mean and sample variance recover unknown population quantities.
04

Population, sample and sampling distribution

Population distribution

The fixed score law from which every penetration score is generated. Its true parameters are μ = 6 and σ² = 2.4.

One sample

The n scores observed by one hacker. From them we compute one sample mean and one sample variance.

Sampling distribution

The distribution obtained by repeating the sampling experiment many times. With m hackers, we get m estimates.

The central idea of inferential statistics: a statistic computed from one sample is random because the sample itself is random. We therefore study the distribution of the statistic across many possible samples.
05

Why do we compute a mean and a variance for each hacker?

Each hacker sees a different random trajectory of scores. Suppose one hacker obtains:

5, 7, 6, 8, 4, 6, 7, 5, ...

Looking at the raw sequence alone is not enough. We need a compact way to summarize two different aspects of the trajectory:

Where is the trajectory centered?

The sample mean tells us the average level of the hacker's scores. It is our estimate of the population mean μ.

How much do the scores fluctuate?

The sample variance measures how dispersed the hacker's scores are around their own sample mean.

Why both?

Two hackers may have the same average score but very different consistency. Mean describes the center; variance describes the spread.

Therefore each trajectory becomes one statistical sample, summarized by two numbers: x̄ and s².
06

Why use Welford here?

A hacker's scores arrive one penetration attempt at a time. We could store all n values and compute mean and variance only at the end, but the previous exercise showed that this is unnecessary.

Welford lets us update the statistics as each score arrives. After the k-th score, we already know the current mean and variance of the first k observations.

1
Measure how surprising the new score is

δ = x − mean

If the new score is above the current mean, δ is positive. If it is below the current mean, δ is negative.

2
Move the mean toward the new observation

mean ← mean + δ / k

The new score does not replace the old mean. It moves it by only a fraction of the distance. As k grows, one new observation has less influence.

3
Update the accumulated spread

M₂ ← M₂ + δ(x − mean)

This keeps track of the total squared deviation needed for the variance, while automatically accounting for the fact that the mean has just changed.

At the end of the trajectory:

Sample mean x̄ = mean This estimates μ.
Sample variance s² = M₂ / (n − 1) This estimates σ².
07

Why do we divide by n − 1 instead of n?

This is an important point. The scores of one hacker are only a sample from a larger population distribution. We do not know the true population mean μ, so we first estimate it using the same sample.

That creates a small bias: the sample mean is chosen from the data themselves, so the observed deviations around that sample mean tend to look slightly smaller than the deviations around the true population mean.

Intuition

The sample mean is the point that best fits the sample itself. Because it is selected from those exact observations, the sample is naturally a little too “comfortable” around its own mean.

Dividing by n − 1 instead of n compensates for this effect. This correction is called Bessel's correction.

s² = M₂ / (n − 1)

The payoff is extremely important: when we repeat the sampling experiment many times, the average of these sample variances equals the true population variance. In symbols, E[s²] = σ².

10

From one hacker to many: what is a sampling distribution?

Up to this point we have followed one hacker. That hacker gives us one sample mean and one sample variance. But if we repeat the same experiment with another hacker, the scores will be different, so the estimates will also be different.

This does not mean one of the hackers made a mistake. It means the statistic itself is random because it depends on a random sample.

Hacker 1→x̄₁, s₁² Hacker 2→x̄₂, s₂² ... Hacker m→x̄ₘ, sₘ²

The collection of all possible values of a statistic across repeated random samples is called its sampling distribution.

We are no longer studying the distribution of individual scores. We are studying the distribution of an estimator.
11

The sampling means

Each hacker produces one sample mean. The histogram below shows how those m sample means vary from hacker to hacker.

Why is the distribution centered on μ? Some hackers will obtain a sample mean above 6 and others below 6. If the sampling process is unbiased, these positive and negative errors balance across many repetitions. That is why E[x̄] = μ.
Why does n matter? Averaging more independent observations smooths out random fluctuations. The exact result is Var(x̄)=σ²/n. So if n becomes four times larger, the variance of the sample mean becomes four times smaller, and its standard deviation becomes two times smaller.
12

The sampling variances

Each hacker also produces one sample variance. These estimates are random too: two hackers can observe different levels of dispersion even though their scores come from exactly the same population.

Why should the average approach σ²? Because we deliberately use the corrected estimator s²=M₂/(n−1). A single hacker may overestimate or underestimate the true variance, but across many independent hackers those errors balance: E[s²]=σ².
Why is the variance estimator more irregular than the mean? Variance depends on squared deviations. Large unusual scores therefore have a stronger effect on s² than they have on the sample mean. This is why the sampling distribution of sample variance is often more asymmetric, especially when n is small.
13

What do the two comparisons tell us?

Average of sample means vs μ

Across many independent hackers, positive and negative sampling errors tend to balance. The average sample mean should therefore be close to 6.

Average of sample variances vs σ²

The unbiased variance estimator fluctuates from sample to sample, but its average over many independent samples should be close to 2.4.

One estimate is not the truth

A single hacker can obtain estimates noticeably different from the population parameters. This is normal sampling variability, not an error in the algorithm.

This is the bridge to inference. We observe a sample statistic, but we want to learn something about an unknown population parameter. Sampling distributions tell us how much uncertainty to expect in that inference.

15

Why increasing n changes the variability

Increasing n does not change the population. Every individual score still comes from exactly the same Binomial(10, 0.6) distribution. What changes is the amount of information available to each hacker.

Sample meanVar(x̄) = σ²/nThe relation is exact. If n becomes four times larger, the variance of the sample mean becomes four times smaller.
Sample variancevariability also decreases with nIts exact variance depends on the population's fourth central moment, but larger samples make the estimate more stable.

For small n, different hackers obtain noticeably different means and variances. As n grows, those estimates become more concentrated around the true parameters.

16

Why is this useful in inferential statistics?

In real problems we usually do not know μ and σ². We observe only one finite sample and use its mean and variance to learn about the population.

Point estimation

The sample mean estimates μ and the sample variance estimates σ². The experiment shows why these are sensible estimators.

Uncertainty

Repeated samples do not give identical answers. Sampling variability tells us how uncertain a sample-based estimate is.

More data, more precision

Larger samples reduce the variability of estimators. This is the basic reason larger studies generally produce more precise conclusions.

Confidence intervals, standard errors and hypothesis tests are all based on this same idea: understand the sampling distribution of a statistic, then use it to quantify how much confidence we should place in the statistic observed from one sample.

18

Conclusions

This experiment starts from a population that we completely control: every score is generated from X ~ Binomial(10,0.6). Because of this choice, we know that the true population parameters are μ = 6 and σ² = 2.4.

The purpose of the homework is not simply to reproduce those numbers numerically. The important question is whether statistics computed from finite random samples behave in a predictable way around those population values.

1. Different hackers obtain different answers

Every hacker samples the same population, but receives a different sequence of scores. Consequently, their sample means and sample variances are not identical.

This difference is called sampling variability. It is not a programming error and it does not mean the estimators are wrong. It is an unavoidable consequence of observing only a finite random sample.

2. The sample mean is centered on μ

Some hackers obtain x̄ > 6, others x̄ < 6. Across many independent hackers, these positive and negative deviations balance.

Therefore the average of the sample means approaches the theoretical mean: E[x̄]=μ=6.

This is why the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of μ.

3. The corrected sample variance targets σ²

For each hacker we use s²=M₂/(n-1), not M₂/n. The n-1 correction compensates for the fact that the same sample was already used to estimate its own mean.

As a result, over repeated samples: E[s²]=σ²=2.4.

One hacker may obtain 2.1 and another 2.8, but the estimator is correctly centered on the population variance.

4. Increasing n does not change the truth

When we increase the number of systems attempted by each hacker, the underlying population remains exactly the same: μ=6 and σ²=2.4.

What changes is the amount of information available to each hacker. Larger samples give more stable estimates of the same population parameters.

5. The sampling mean becomes more concentrated

For the mean we have the exact result Var(x̄)=σ²/n. Therefore, as n increases, the distribution of sample means becomes narrower around 6.

This means that two hackers using large samples are much more likely to obtain similar estimates than two hackers using very small samples.

6. The sample variance also becomes more stable

The variance estimator has more complicated sampling behavior because it depends on squared deviations, so unusual observations can affect it strongly when n is small.

As n grows, those accidental fluctuations matter less and the distribution of s² becomes progressively more concentrated around 2.4.

What does this teach us about inferential statistics? We rarely know population parameters directly. Instead, we observe a sample and compute statistics such as x̄ and s². Their usefulness comes from knowing how they behave under repeated sampling: where they are centered and how variable they are.

This is exactly why concepts such as standard error, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are possible. They do not assume that one sample gives the exact truth. They quantify how much a statistic is expected to fluctuate from sample to sample.

Final interpretation. More data do not magically change the population and do not guarantee that a single estimate equals the true parameter exactly. What larger samples do is reduce uncertainty: the sampling distributions become tighter, so our estimates become more precise and therefore more reliable for drawing conclusions about the population.
