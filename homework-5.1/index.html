Welford’s Recurrence Formulas

Understanding how mean and variance can be updated online, one observation at a time.

01

Where does the problem come from?

In a traditional statistics exercise we usually receive the complete dataset immediately. If the observations are 4, 7, 13, 16, 5, we can sum them, divide by the number of observations, and obtain the mean.

But real data often arrive gradually: sensor readings, network traffic, server response times, measurements from an experiment, or values produced by a simulation.

new observation→update statistics→wait for next value

Recomputing everything from the beginning after every new observation would work, but it would repeatedly revisit information we have already processed.

Goal: summarize the past with a small amount of information and update that summary whenever a new observation arrives.
02

The official recurrence formula for the mean

Suppose we have already processed n − 1 observations and their mean is x̄ₙ₋₁. A new observation xₙ arrives.

x̄ₙ = x̄ₙ₋₁ + (xₙ − x̄ₙ₋₁)/n

This formula says something very intuitive: start from the old mean and move it toward the new observation.

x̄ₙ₋₁ the mean before the new observation arrives
xₙ − x̄ₙ₋₁ the distance between the new observation and the old mean
/ n only a fraction of that distance is used to move the mean

We usually give the difference a short name:

δ = xₙ − x̄ₙ₋₁

Therefore the same recurrence becomes x̄ₙ = x̄ₙ₋₁ + δ/n.

04

Why mean alone is not enough

The mean tells us where the center of a dataset is, but it does not tell us how the values are distributed around that center.

Dataset A 9, 10, 11 Mean = 10
Dataset B 0, 10, 20 Mean = 10

Both datasets have mean 10, yet Dataset A is tightly concentrated around 10 while Dataset B is widely spread. We therefore need a second quantity that describes dispersion.

Mean asks: where is the center?
Variance asks: how far, in general, are the observations from that center?
05

The official variance formula, explained slowly

For a population, variance is defined as:

σ² = (1/n) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ − x̄)²

This formula is easier to understand if we read it from the inside out.

xᵢ − x̄ how far one observation is from the mean
(xᵢ − x̄)² square that distance so negative and positive deviations cannot cancel
Σ add the squared deviations of all observations
1/n average those squared deviations

Why does a negative sign become positive?

Suppose the mean is 10 and one observation is 7. Its deviation is:

Deviation7 − 10 = −3
→ square it →
Squared deviation(−3)² = 9

The value is below the mean, so the deviation is negative. But variance is interested in distance, not direction. Squaring removes the sign because multiplying two negative numbers gives a positive result:

(−3)² = (−3)(−3) = +9

The same happens above the mean. If the observation is 13:

Deviation13 − 10 = +3
→ square it →
Squared deviation(+3)² = 9

So observations equally far below and above the mean contribute equally to variance. Their direction is different, but their distance from the center is the same.

07

From variance to Welford

The classical variance formula assumes that all observations are available. Welford’s problem is different: values arrive one at a time.

Welford keeps the accumulated sum of squared deviations in a quantity commonly called M₂. It is the running version of the numerator of the variance.

M₂ = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²

Once M₂ is known, the variance is easy:

Population variance σ² = M₂ / n Use when the observations are the whole population.
Sample variance s² = M₂ / (n − 1) Use when the observations are a sample from a larger population.
08

Welford’s official recurrence formulas

When a new observation xₙ arrives, Welford updates the mean and M₂ in three conceptual steps.

1
Measure the difference from the old mean

δ = xₙ − x̄ₙ₋₁

2
Update the mean

x̄ₙ = x̄ₙ₋₁ + δ/n

3
Update the accumulated squared deviation

M₂,ₙ = M₂,ₙ₋₁ + δ(xₙ − x̄ₙ)

The third formula may look unusual because it multiplies two differences: one measured from the old mean and one measured from the new mean.

This is precisely what corrects the accumulated spread after the center itself has moved. We do not need to revisit every old observation.

The three running quantities are therefore: n, x̄, and M₂. Together they summarize everything needed for mean and variance.

What should we remember?

The mean describes the center.New observations move that center gradually.
Variance describes the spread.It measures squared distance from the mean.
Squaring removes direction.Negative and positive deviations become positive contributions.
Welford makes the process online.Only n, mean and M₂ need to be maintained.
