Statistical Independence

Can knowing one variable help us predict another? An interactive experiment with a contingency table, conditional probabilities, expected frequencies and Pearson's chi-square test.

From a bivariate dataset to a testable conclusion.

01 · What does independence mean?

In probability theory, two random variables X and F are independent when learning the value of one does not change the probability distribution of the other. For every pair of categories with positive conditioning probability:

P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x) · P(F = f)

Equivalently, P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f). If the conditional distribution of F changes across categories of X, the variables are associated, rather than independent.

In statistics, the probabilities are unknown. We observe a finite sample, estimate probabilities using relative frequencies, and test whether the observed departures from independence are greater than we would plausibly expect from random sampling. Even independent population variables will generally not produce perfectly factorized sample frequencies.

Null hypothesis · H₀

X and F are independent in the population. Any deviations in our sample are due to sampling variability.

Alternative · H₁

X and F are associated in the population. At least one joint probability differs from the product of its marginals.

02 · Build the bivariate dataset

Our illustrative, synthetic dataset concerns students' study method (X) and exam outcome (F). Each observation is one student belonging to exactly one category of each variable. The values are invented for teaching: they are not evidence about real students.

Research question and purpose

Does the distribution of exam results differ between students who use different study methods? We want to understand whether knowing a student’s study method provides information about their exam outcome. This is a test of association, not a study of which method causes better performance.

What does each variable and dataset column mean?

The built-in scenarios contain different numbers of fictional students: 250 in the associated example and 180 in the independence control. The study-method groups also have different sizes. Each CSV row represents one student and has exactly two columns. The first, X (Study method), is a categorical explanatory or grouping variable: Guided means studying with structured instruction or guidance, while Self-directed means organizing study independently. The second, F (Exam outcome), is a categorical response variable with three ordered labels: Low, Medium, and High, representing relative levels of exam performance. These are illustrative categories, not numeric grades or predefined score cutoffs.

What do we expect to find?

Under the null hypothesis (H₀), exam outcomes should have roughly the same proportions within both study-method groups: for example, the percentage with a High outcome should not systematically change between Guided and Self-directed. Expected cell counts are obtained from row and column totals, not assumed to be the same in every cell. Under the alternative (H₁), at least one outcome proportion differs across study methods, so the observed contingency table departs from what independence predicts.

Scenario A — association (250 students): 150 use Guided study and 100 use Self-directed study. Their outcomes differ: Guided has 75 Low, 45 Medium and 30 High results; Self-directed has 15 Low, 35 Medium and 50 High results. We expect noticeably different within-group outcome percentages, a large chi-square statistic and evidence against independence. Scenario B — independence control (180 students): 80 use Guided study and 100 use Self-directed study. Their outcomes are 16/32/32 and 20/40/40, respectively. Despite unequal group sizes, both have the same outcome percentages (20% Low, 40% Medium, 40% High), so we expect the observed counts to match the independence expectations exactly (χ² = 0, p = 1). These are deliberately constructed examples, not real-world findings.

Experimental design: what stays the same, and what changes?

Both datasets record exactly the same two categorical variables for each student, but the sample sizes, numbers of students in each method and outcome counts change. This comparison isolates the key lesson: independence is about conditional proportions, not equal numbers of students. An outcome count of 40 means something different in a group of 80 than in a group of 100. That is why we compare percentages within each row before interpreting the test.

From observations to a contingency table

We count how many times each pair (X = xᵢ, F = fⱼ) occurs. These counts are called observed frequencies, denoted Oᵢⱼ. Row totals summarize X, column totals summarize F, and the grand total is n.

03 · Observed, marginal and conditional frequencies

The contingency table is built programmatically by counting each (X, F) pair. Its last row and column contain the marginal frequencies.

Marginal relative frequencies estimate probabilities for one variable without conditioning on the other: p̂(X=xᵢ) = Oᵢ· / n and p̂(F=fⱼ) = O·ⱼ / n. Joint relative frequencies divide each observed count by n.

04 · Expected frequencies under independence

Under H₀, the estimated probability of being in cell (i, j) is the product of its two marginal probabilities. Multiplying by sample size n gives the expected count:

Eᵢⱼ = (row totalᵢ × column totalⱼ) / n

What is the chi-square distance, and why do we measure it?

The expression “chi-square distance” is often used informally here for the Pearson chi-square statistic: a discrepancy score between the observed contingency table and the expected table computed under independence. Each cell contributes its squared observed-minus-expected difference, scaled by the expected count. Scaling matters: a difference of ten students is relatively more substantial when we expected ten than when we expected one hundred.

What does the score tell us? A value of 0 means observed and expected counts match in every cell. Larger values mean greater departure from the pattern predicted by independence. The score is not a probability, a percentage, nor a measure of causal influence; it also depends on the sample size. Mathematically, χ² is a weighted sum of squared differences, not an ordinary geometric distance between two points. Some authors call √χ² the chi-square distance, so we explicitly report Pearson’s χ² statistic throughout.

Worked intuition: in Scenario A, the Guided–Low cell contains 75 students, but independence predicts (150 × 90) / 250 = 54. Its contribution is (75 − 54)² / 54 ≈ 8.17. The test adds the contributions from all six cells. Scenario B has proportional outcome distributions, so every cell contributes zero.

Pearson's chi-square statistic

For every cell, compare observed and expected counts. Square their difference and divide by the expected count. The total measures the overall departure from independence; large values argue against H₀. Yet χ² alone is insufficient to make a statistical decision: we compare it with a chi-square reference distribution using the degrees of freedom and p-value.

χ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ (Oᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ

How do we interpret the distance? Under H₀, we expect a modest positive χ² from sampling noise in most random samples. With enough independent observations and sufficiently large expected counts, we use the chi-square distribution to judge whether the observed score is unusually large. When conditions for the chi-square approximation are reasonable, the reference distribution has (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom. We compute a right-tail p-value, P(χ²df ≥ χ²observed), which measures how surprising this much departure would be if H₀ were true.

Association is not causation

Even a statistically significant relationship does not establish that the study method caused the exam result. Confounders such as prior preparation, available time, and motivation could influence both variables. The data are observational and synthetic; a causal conclusion would need a defensible research design, typically including random assignment or careful control of confounding factors.

What have we learned?

Probability independence is a property of the population distribution. Statistical testing uses a sample to judge the evidence against that property. The contingency table makes the empirical relationship visible; expected counts and the chi-square statistic quantify departures from independence; the p-value supports an inferential decision, subject to assumptions and uncertainty.
